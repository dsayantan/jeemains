Chemistry

Equilibrium is dynamic, and K depends only on temperature

At equilibrium both reactions are still running, just at equal rates. The constant K is fixed by temperature alone: changing a concentration or the pressure shifts the position but not the constant, and a catalyst changes neither — it only shortens the wait.

Worked through

Example

For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, what happens to K if the pressure is raised?

Answer: K is unchanged; the yield of NH₃ rises.

Pressure shifts the position towards fewer gas moles, i.e. to the right, but K is a function of T only.

Chemistry

Le Chatelier, in three moves

Raise a concentration and the system consumes it. Raise the pressure and it moves to the side with fewer gas molecules. Raise the temperature and it moves in the endothermic direction. Adding an inert gas at constant volume does nothing at all, which is a favourite trap.

Worked through

Example

2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ is exothermic. How do high pressure and high temperature each affect the yield?

Answer: High pressure raises the yield, high temperature lowers it.

Fewer moles on the right, so pressure helps; heat is a product, so temperature hurts.

Chemistry

pH, and the weak acid shortcut

pH = −log[H⁺], and for water pH + pOH = 14 at 298 K. A strong acid dissociates completely so [H⁺] is just its concentration; a weak one needs [H⁺] = √(K_aC). Below about 10⁻⁶ M even a strong acid needs water's own H⁺ included, so the pH of 10⁻⁸ M HCl is just under 7, never 8.

Worked through

Example

Find the pH of 0.01 M acetic acid, K_a = 1.8×10⁻⁵.

Answer: pH ≈ 3.37.

[H⁺] = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 10⁻²) = √(1.8×10⁻⁷) ≈ 4.24×10⁻⁴.

Chemistry

Buffers

A weak acid with its salt resists pH change, because either added acid or added base is mopped up by one of the two components. pH = pK_a + log([salt]/[acid]), so the buffer works best when the two are equal — and diluting it leaves the pH almost untouched, since only the ratio appears.

Worked through

Example

An acid of pK_a 4.76 is mixed with an equal concentration of its sodium salt. Find the pH.

Answer: 4.76.

log(1) = 0, so pH = pK_a.

Chemistry

Solubility product and the common ion effect

For a sparingly soluble salt, K_sp is the product of the ion concentrations at saturation. Adding a common ion pushes the equilibrium back and the solubility falls; precipitation begins as soon as the ionic product exceeds K_sp.

Worked through

S² = K_sp, so S = √(1.8×10⁻¹⁰).

Chemistry

Salt hydrolysis

A salt of a strong acid and strong base gives a neutral solution; weak acid with strong base gives basic; strong acid with weak base gives acidic. With both weak, whichever is the weaker partner decides, through pH = 7 + ½(pK_a − pK_b).

Worked through

Is a solution of sodium acetate acidic, basic or neutral?

Answer: Basic.

It is the salt of a weak acid and a strong base, so the acetate hydrolyses.

Chemistry

Werner's two valencies

The primary valency is the oxidation state and is satisfied by anions; the secondary valency is the coordination number and is satisfied by ligands held in fixed positions in space. That geometric fixing is what makes isomerism possible — and it is why only the ions outside the square brackets are precipitated by AgNO₃.

Example

How many moles of AgCl does one mole of [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂ give with excess AgNO₃?

Answer: 2 moles.

Only the two chlorides outside the coordination sphere are free.

Chemistry

Crystal field splitting, and high versus low spin

Ligands approaching a metal ion split the five d orbitals. If the splitting Δ₀ is bigger than the energy cost of pairing, the electrons pair up — low spin, fewer unpaired electrons, smaller magnetic moment. A weak-field ligand leaves Δ₀ small and the electrons spread out — high spin. Tetrahedral complexes have Δ_t = (4/9)Δ₀ and so are always high spin.

Worked through

Example

[Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ has one unpaired electron. What does that tell you about CN⁻?

Answer: CN⁻ is a strong-field ligand, and the complex is low spin.

Fe³⁺ is d⁵; one unpaired electron means the other four are paired, so Δ₀ beat the pairing energy.

Chemistry

The chelate effect

A bidentate or polydentate ligand grips the metal in a ring and gives a far more stable complex than the same number of separate ligands would. The reason is largely entropy: one chelating molecule displaces several monodentate ones, so the number of free particles rises.

Worked through

Example

Why is [Ni(en)₃]²⁺ more stable than [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺?

Answer: The chelate effect — an entropy advantage.

Three en molecules replace six NH₃, so the number of free particles increases and ΔS is positive.

The carbonyl group is an electrophile

Oxygen pulls electron density off the carbon, leaving it open to nucleophilic attack. Aldehydes react faster than ketones because they have only one electron-releasing alkyl group instead of two, and are less crowded. Hence HCHO > CH₃CHO > acetone > acetophenone > benzophenone.

Chemistry

Why carboxylic acids are the strongest of the common organics

Losing the proton leaves a carboxylate whose negative charge is shared equally over two oxygens, so the anion is far more stable than a phenoxide or an alkoxide. Order: carboxylic acid > phenol > water > alcohol. Electron-withdrawing groups near the acid group strengthen it further, and their effect falls off sharply with distance.

Worked through

Example

Arrange in increasing acidity: CH₃COOH, ClCH₂COOH, Cl₃CCOOH.

Answer: CH₃COOH < ClCH₂COOH < Cl₃CCOOH.

More chlorines mean more −I stabilisation of the anion.

Each line shows how the free energy of formation of an oxide varies with temperature. A metal whose line lies below another's will reduce that other's oxide. Carbon's line slopes downward, so above a certain temperature it can reduce almost anything — but not aluminium or magnesium, which is why those are won electrolytically.

Worked through

Example

Why is aluminium not extracted by carbon reduction?

Answer: Because carbon cannot reduce alumina; electrolysis is used instead.

The Al₂O₃ line lies below carbon's at every practical temperature.

Chemistry

SN1 and SN2 differ in everything that matters

SN2 is one step: the nucleophile pushes in as the halide leaves, the centre inverts, and the rate depends on both concentrations. It prefers methyl and primary halides. SN1 is two steps through a carbocation, so it racemises, depends only on the halide's concentration, and prefers tertiary.

Worked through

Example

Which reacts faster with OH⁻ by SN2, 1-bromobutane or 2-bromo-2-methylpropane?

Answer: 1-Bromobutane.

SN2 is blocked by steric crowding at the reaction centre.

Chemistry

Why aryl halides are so unreactive

The C–X bond has partial double-bond character from resonance with the ring, the carbon is sp² and holds its electrons more tightly, and the ring's π cloud repels the incoming nucleophile. Substitution needs forcing conditions, or strong electron-withdrawing groups ortho and para to the halogen.

Worked through

Example

Why does chlorobenzene not react with aqueous NaOH at room temperature?

Answer: The bond is too strong; the Dow process needs 623 K and 300 atm.

Resonance gives the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character.

The compressibility factor

Z = pV/nRT is 1 for an ideal gas. Below 1 the attractive forces dominate and the gas is more compressible than ideal; above 1 the molecular volume dominates. At very high pressure every gas has Z > 1.

Worked through

Answer: Greater than 1.

The repulsive, finite-volume term takes over.
