The sign convention, and why it matters

Everything is measured from the pole of the mirror or the optical centre of the lens, and distances measured in the direction the light travels are positive. A real object therefore sits at negative u. Get this wrong and every subsequent formula returns a plausible but wrong number, which is why the convention is worth fixing in your head before any of the formulae.

Worked through

Example

An object is 30 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 20 cm. Where is the image?

Answer: 60 cm in front of the mirror — real, inverted and magnified.

Using 1/v + 1/u = 1/f with magnitudes and the real-is-positive habit: 1/v = 1/20 − 1/30 = 1/60.

Physics

Image formation by a convex lens

Two rays fix the image. One arrives parallel to the axis and leaves through the far focus; one goes straight through the optical centre undeviated. Where they cross is the image. As the object comes in from infinity towards F the image moves out from F towards infinity, growing all the while; inside F it flips to virtual, erect and magnified, which is the magnifying-glass case.

Worked through

Example

A 2 cm object stands 15 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the image.

Answer: 30 cm beyond the lens, real, inverted, 4 cm tall.

With a real object 1/v = 1/f − 1/|u| = 1/10 − 1/15 = 1/30, so v = 30 cm; m = v/u = 30/15 = 2.

Physics

Mirrors

A concave mirror behaves much like a convex lens and a convex mirror like a concave lens, with f = R/2. The useful shortcut is that a convex mirror and a concave lens can only ever give a virtual, erect, diminished image — so in a multiple-choice question those options settle themselves.

Worked through

Example

Why does a car's wing mirror carry the warning that objects are closer than they appear?

Answer: Because a convex mirror always diminishes.

It is convex, so the image is always diminished, and a smaller image reads to the eye as a more distant one.

Physics

Refraction and total internal reflection

Light bends towards the normal on entering a denser medium. Going the other way it bends away, and past the critical angle it cannot get out at all — it is reflected entirely. This needs both conditions: denser to rarer, and an angle greater than C. It is what makes optical fibres, diamonds and mirages work.

Worked through

Example

What is the critical angle for glass of refractive index 1.5 in air?

Answer: C ≈ 41.8°.

sin C = 1/1.5 = 0.667.

Physics

The prism

A prism deviates light twice, once at each face, and the total deviation δ = i + e − A. As the angle of incidence is increased the deviation first falls, reaches a minimum when the ray passes symmetrically (i = e), then rises again. Because n depends on wavelength, violet deviates most and the beam spreads into a spectrum.

Worked through

Example

A prism of angle 60° gives minimum deviation 30°. Find n.

Answer: n = 1.414.

n = sin((A + δₘ)/2)/sin(A/2) = sin45°/sin30° = 0.7071/0.5.

From the papers

Consider an equilateral prism (refractive index √2). A ray of light is incident on its one surface at a certain angle i. If the emergent ray is found to graze along the other surface then the angle of refraction at the incident surface is close to ____.

  1. 15°
  2. 20°
  3. 40°

Answer: (1) 15°

Grazing emergence means the ray leaves at 90°, so it strikes the second face at exactly the critical angle: sin C = 1/√2 gives C = 45°. In a prism r₁ + r₂ = A, so r₁ = 60° − 45°.

Physics

Optical instruments

Every instrument here is two lenses and one idea: magnification is the ratio of the angle the image subtends to the angle the object would subtend unaided. For a telescope that gives f₀/fₑ, so you want a long objective and a short eyepiece; for a microscope both should be short. Reading which adjustment is meant — image at infinity or at the near point D — decides whether the extra (1 + D/f) factor appears.

Worked through

Example

A telescope has f₀ = 100 cm and fₑ = 5 cm. Find its magnifying power and tube length in normal adjustment.

Answer: M = 20, tube length 105 cm.

M = f₀/fₑ = 20; L = f₀ + fₑ = 105 cm.

From the papers

  2. Increase the refractive index of the medium between the object and objective lens
  3. Decrease the focal length of the eye piece
  4. Decrease the diameter of the objective lens

Apparent depth

Why a pool looks shallower than it is. The shift is how much the object appears to rise, and it depends only on the thickness of the medium and its index — not on where you stand, provided you look nearly straight down.

What a capacitor stores

A capacitor holds equal and opposite charges on two conductors, and C = Q/V is fixed by geometry alone — for parallel plates, ε₀A/d. The energy ½CV² is stored in the field between the plates, not on them, which is why the energy density ½ε₀E² is the more fundamental statement.

Worked through

Example

A 10 μF capacitor is charged to 100 V. How much energy does it hold?

Answer: 0.05 J.

U = ½CV² = ½ × 10×10⁻⁶ × 100².

From the papers

A parallel plate capacitor with width 4 cm, length 8 cm and separation between the plates of 4 mm is connected to a battery of 20 V. A dielectric slab of dielectric constant 5 having length 1 cm, width 4 cm and thickness 4 mm is inserted between the plates of parallel plate capacitor. The electrostatic energy of this system will be ________ ε₀ J. (Where ε₀ is the permittivity of free space)

JEE Main 2022-07-27, Shift 2

Answer: 240

The slab fills the full gap over 1 cm of the 8 cm length, so the two regions are capacitors in parallel. Air part: ε₀(4×7×10⁻⁴)/(4×10⁻³) = 0.7ε₀. Slab part: 5ε₀(4×1×10⁻⁴)/(4×10⁻³) = 0.5ε₀. So C = 1.2ε₀ and U = ½CV² = ½(1.2ε₀)(400).

A slab that spans the whole gap but only part of the area splits the capacitor in parallel; a slab that spans the whole area but only part of the gap splits it in series. Deciding which is the entire question.

Physics

Battery connected or disconnected — the whole chapter turns on this

If the battery stays connected, V is held fixed and the charge adjusts. If it is removed, Q is trapped and the voltage adjusts. Insert a dielectric with the battery on and C, Q and U all rise; insert it with the battery off and C rises while V, E and U all fall by the factor K. Almost every question in this chapter is one of these two cases in disguise.

Worked through

Example

A charged, isolated capacitor has a dielectric of K = 4 slid in. What happens to the stored energy?

Answer: The energy falls to a quarter — the slab is pulled in, doing work.

Q is fixed, so U = Q²/2C and C has gone up four times.

Series and parallel, the other way round

Capacitors combine opposite to resistors: parallel adds, series adds reciprocals. The consequence worth remembering is that in series the smallest capacitor takes the largest share of the voltage, which is the reverse of the intuition carried over from resistors.

Charging through a resistor

Charge grows as q = CV(1 − e^(−t/RC)). The product RC is the time constant: after one RC the capacitor is 63% charged, after 5RC essentially full. In a steady d.c. circuit no current flows in a capacitor branch at all, so for 'long after the switch is closed' questions you can simply delete that branch and read off the voltage across whatever it bridges.

Worked through

R = 1 MΩ and C = 2 μF. How long to reach 63% of full charge?

Answer: 2 s.

That is one time constant, τ = RC = 10⁶ × 2×10⁻⁶.

Physics

Energy lost on sharing

Join two charged capacitors and charge flows until they share a common voltage. Energy is always lost, however slowly you do it, and the loss C₁C₂(V₁ − V₂)²/2(C₁ + C₂) goes into the connecting wires as heat and radiation. A question asking 'is energy conserved' here is asking whether you know that it is not.

Worked through

Example

Taking a ferromagnetic round a full cycle traces a loop whose area is the energy lost per cycle. Soft iron has a thin loop and is used for transformer cores and electromagnets; steel has a fat one and makes permanent magnets.
